Corporate Services > Transfer of Shares

A transfer of shares service is how a small limited company moves shares from one person to another: when a shareholder sells their stake, gives shares to a family member, or brings in a new co-owner. The transfer does not create new shares, and it does not change the company's total share capital. It simply changes who owns the shares that already exist.

What Is a Transfer of Shares?

A transfer of shares is the legal process of moving ownership of shares from one person, the transferor, to another person, the transferee. The shares themselves do not change: the same number of shares, of the same class, with the same rights, simply belong to someone new. The company's total number of shares stays exactly the same, and its share capital does not change.

Transfers happen for practical reasons. A shareholder may sell their shares to a new investor, or to an existing shareholder who wants a larger stake. A founder may give shares to a spouse or a child as part of succession planning. A shareholder who is leaving the business may transfer their shares back to the company or to the remaining shareholders.

Whatever the reason, the transfer is a transaction between the seller and the buyer, not a decision by the company alone. The company records the transfer in its own registers, and it may need to approve it, but the shares move because the two parties have agreed that they should, and because the proper form has been completed and signed.

What Is the Difference Between Transferring Shares and Issuing Shares?

Transferring shares and issuing shares sound similar, but they are different transactions, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes small companies make.

Issuing shares, also called allotting shares, creates new shares. The company's total number of shares goes up, and its share capital increases. An issue needs authority to allot, a board resolution, and a return of allotment, form SH01, filed with Companies House within one month.

Transferring shares does not create anything new. An existing shareholder sells or gives their shares to someone else, and the total number of shares stays exactly the same. A transfer is recorded on a stock transfer form and in the register of members, and it does not need an SH01, because no new shares have been allotted.

For a small business, the practical point is simple: if the shares already exist and are changing hands, it is a transfer. If the company is creating shares that did not exist before, it is an issue. Getting that distinction right matters, because the wrong form, filed in the wrong place, does not transfer the shares, and a transfer that has not been properly recorded leaves the buyer without clear title to what they have bought.

What Are the 6 Checks to Make Before You Transfer Shares?

  1. Do the articles of association allow the transfer? Check the articles and any shareholders' agreement first. They may restrict transfers, require the directors' consent, or give the existing shareholders a right of first refusal.
  2. Do the existing shareholders have pre-emption rights? Many companies give existing shareholders the first chance to buy shares that are being sold, in proportion to their existing holdings. If pre-emption rights apply, offer the shares to them first, or get their written waiver.
  3. Are the shares fully paid? The stock transfer form J30 is for fully paid shares. If the shares are unpaid or only partly paid, a different form, J10, is needed, and the buyer takes on the liability to pay the unpaid amount.
  4. Is the price agreed, and is it the right price? Agree the consideration, how much the buyer will pay, and record it in writing. If the price is below market value, HMRC may still charge Stamp Duty on the market value, and the seller may have a Capital Gains Tax bill on the difference.
  5. Has the buyer been checked? The company should know who it is bringing in as a shareholder. Check the buyer's identity, and consider whether they will become a person with significant control, which must be reported to Companies House within 14 days.
  6. Do you have the share certificate and the company's registers? The seller's share certificate will need to be cancelled and a new one issued to the buyer, and the register of members must be ready to be updated. If the certificate has been lost, a replacement must be arranged first.

What Is the Stock Transfer Form (J30)?

The stock transfer form, usually the J30, is the legal instrument that moves the shares. It records the company name, the class and number of shares being transferred, the seller (the transferor), the buyer (the transferee), and the consideration paid. Both parties sign it, and it is the document that the company relies on to update its register of members.

Section 770(1) of the Companies Act 2006 requires the buyer to deliver a proper instrument of transfer to the company before the company updates its register. Without that form, the company cannot register the transfer, and the buyer is not yet the legal owner of the shares, even if the money has already been paid.

The form is not filed with Companies House. It stays in the company's own records, together with the share certificates and the board minutes that approved the transfer. Companies House only sees the change later, when it is reported on the next confirmation statement, unless the transfer changes who qualifies as a person with significant control, in which case Companies House must be told within 14 days.

There are two details to get right on the form. The first is the consideration: the amount the buyer is paying, or a statement that no consideration is being paid, where the shares are a gift. The second is the certificate: if the consideration is £1,000 or less, the form must carry a certificate stating that the transaction is exempt from Stamp Duty. If the consideration is more than £1,000, the form must be sent to HMRC for stamping, and the Stamp Duty paid, before the company registers the transfer.

How Do You Transfer Shares Step by Step?

First, check the articles of association and any shareholders' agreement, using the checks above. If the transfer is restricted, get the directors' consent or the existing shareholders' waiver before going any further. If pre-emption rights apply, offer the shares to the existing shareholders and wait for their response, or get their written waiver.

Next, agree the terms in writing: how many shares are being transferred, of which class, for what consideration, and on what date. Then complete the stock transfer form (J30), with the company name, the share details, the seller and the buyer, and the consideration. Both parties sign it. If the consideration is more than £1,000, send the form to HMRC and pay the Stamp Duty, and wait for the form to come back stamped before the company registers the transfer.

Once the stamped form, or the exempt form, where no duty is due, is delivered to the company, the directors approve the transfer at a board meeting, or by written resolution, and the company updates its register of members. The seller's name is removed, the buyer's name is entered, and the transfer is dated on the day the register is updated. Legal ownership passes at that moment: the buyer becomes a shareholder when their name is entered in the register, not when the money was paid or the form was signed.

Finally, cancel the seller's share certificate and issue a new one to the buyer, for the shares they now hold. Keep the completed stock transfer form, the board minute approving the transfer, and the old and new certificates with the company's statutory records. The board has up to two months from the date the transfer documents are lodged to approve or refuse the transfer.

How Much Stamp Duty Do You Pay on a Share Transfer?

Stamp Duty is charged at 0.5% of the consideration, the amount the buyer pays for the shares, where the consideration is more than £1,000. The amount is rounded up to the nearest £5. A transfer of £10,000 worth of shares, for example, incurs £50 in Stamp Duty. A transfer of £10,100 worth of shares incurs £55, because 0.5% of £10,100 is £50.50, rounded up to the nearest £5.

Transfers of £1,000 or less are exempt from Stamp Duty, but the exemption must be certified on the stock transfer form. A gift of shares, where no consideration is paid, is usually exempt from Stamp Duty, although the seller may still have a Capital Gains Tax bill on the market value of the shares.

The buyer is responsible for paying the Stamp Duty, and the form must reach HMRC within 30 days of the date the seller signed it. The buyer calculates the duty, pays it through HMRC's online service, and sends the signed form to HMRC. If the duty is not paid within 30 days, interest and penalties can follow, and the company should not register the transfer until the form has been stamped.

There is one point that catches people out: if the price paid is below the market value of the shares, HMRC may charge Stamp Duty on the market value instead of the price paid. A sale to a connected person, a family member, or another company in the same group, at an artificially low price does not reduce the duty.

What Must the Directors Do When Shares Are Transferred?

The directors' role is to check the transfer, approve it, and make sure the company's records are updated. They are not a party to the sale, but they are responsible for the register, and the register is the legal record of who owns the company.

The directors should first check that the transfer is allowed under the articles of association and any shareholders' agreement. If the articles require the directors' consent, the directors must give it, and record that consent in a board minute. If the articles give the directors a discretion to refuse a transfer, they must exercise that discretion properly.

The directors should also check that the stock transfer form is properly completed and signed, that any Stamp Duty has been paid or the exemption certified, and that the seller's share certificate is available to be cancelled. Once the transfer is approved, the directors must update the register of members on the day the transfer is registered, and issue a new share certificate to the buyer.

The directors should also check whether the transfer changes who qualifies as a person with significant control. Anyone who holds 25% or more of the shares or voting rights, or who can appoint or remove a majority of the directors, is a person with significant control, and the company's PSC register must be updated and Companies House told within 14 days of the change.

How Is a Share Transfer Reported to Companies House?

A share transfer is not filed with Companies House directly. There is no standalone share transfer filing, and the stock transfer form itself is not sent to Companies House. The transfer happens between the parties, on paper, the company updates its own records, and Companies House only sees the change later, on the next annual confirmation statement.

The confirmation statement, form CS01, is filed at least once every 12 months, and it confirms the company's registered details, including its shareholders and its people with significant control. When the next confirmation statement is filed after a transfer, it will show the new shareholder and the shares they hold. The online filing fee for a confirmation statement is £50, and the paper fee is £110.

The exception is a change in the people with significant control. If the transfer creates a new person with significant control, or removes one, or changes the nature of someone's control, the company must notify Companies House within 14 days of updating its PSC register, using the PSC forms (PSC01 to PSC09).

What Tax Should the Seller Expect on a Share Transfer?

The seller's tax is separate from the buyer's Stamp Duty, and it is usually the larger bill. When a shareholder sells shares, they may make a capital gain, the difference between what they paid for the shares and what they sold them for, and that gain is charged to Capital Gains Tax.

The gain is worked out by taking the sale price, deducting the cost of the shares and any allowable costs of the sale, and applying the annual exempt amount. Business Asset Disposal Relief, formerly Entrepreneurs' Relief, may reduce the rate to 10% on the first £1 million of qualifying gains, where the seller meets the conditions. But the conditions are strict, and they should be checked before the sale, not after it.

A gift of shares is also a disposal for Capital Gains Tax, even though no money changes hands: the gain is worked out on the market value of the shares at the date of the gift. The seller must report the gain on their Self Assessment return and pay the tax by the deadline.

What Happens If a Share Transfer Is Done Wrong?

If a share transfer is done wrong, the buyer may not own the shares they have paid for. The commonest mistakes are a stock transfer form that has not been signed by both parties, a transfer that was registered before the Stamp Duty was paid, a transfer that the articles of association did not allow, and a register of members that was never updated after the transfer was approved.

A transfer that has not been registered is not complete in law. The seller remains the shareholder on the register, and the buyer has only a contractual claim against the seller. A transfer that ignored the articles can also be challenged. If the articles gave the existing shareholders a right of first refusal, and the shares were sold to an outsider without offering them first, the existing shareholders can ask a court to set the transfer aside.

The practical point is simple: do the checks first, complete the form properly, pay the Stamp Duty where it is due, get the directors' approval, and update the register on the day the transfer is registered.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between transferring shares and issuing shares?

Transferring shares moves existing shares from one person to another, and the total number of shares stays the same. Issuing shares creates new shares, so the total goes up. A transfer uses the stock transfer form J30. An issue uses Form SH01.

Is Stamp Duty due on every share transfer?

No. Stamp Duty is charged at 0.5% where the consideration is more than £1,000, rounded up to the nearest £5. Transfers of £1,000 or less are exempt, but the exemption must be certified on the form. A gift of shares is usually exempt from Stamp Duty.

When does the buyer legally own the shares?

The buyer becomes a shareholder when their name is entered in the register of members, not when the money was paid or the form was signed. Until the register is updated, the transfer is not complete in law.

Do I need to tell Companies House about a share transfer?

Not directly. There is no standalone share transfer filing. Companies House sees the change on the next confirmation statement. The exception is a change in the people with significant control, which must be reported within 14 days.
