Online Self Assessment tax returns are how a small business owner reports income that HMRC has not already taxed: self-employed profits, rental income, dividends, capital gains and other untaxed income, and pays the tax owed on it. For most people who need one, the return is filed online through HMRC, and the deadline is the same every year: 31 January for the tax year that ended the previous April.

What Is a Self Assessment Tax Return?

A Self Assessment tax return is the form HMRC uses to collect Income Tax that has not already been taken through PAYE. If you are employed, your employer deducts tax from your wages before you are paid. If you are self-employed, a landlord, a company director with untaxed income, or someone who has made a capital gain, nobody has deducted the tax for you, so you have to report the income yourself, work out the tax, and pay it.

The return covers one tax year, which runs from 6 April to the following 5 April. The return for the 2025/26 tax year, for example, covers income earned between 6 April 2025 and 5 April 2026, and it must be filed online by 31 January 2027.

Self Assessment is not a tax on its own. It is a way of reporting income so that the right amount of Income Tax, and in many cases Class 2 and Class 4 National Insurance, can be charged. The return also covers Capital Gains Tax on assets you have sold, and it is where you claim the reliefs and allowances you are entitled to.

Who Needs to File a Self Assessment Tax Return?

You need to file a Self Assessment return if HMRC has sent you a notice to file one, or if you have untaxed income that HMRC needs to know about. The most common reason for a small business owner is self-employment: if you were self-employed as a sole trader and earned more than £1,000 in the tax year, you must file. That £1,000 is the trading allowance. Earn less than that from self-employment and you do not need to tell HMRC about it.

Other common reasons include being a landlord with rental income, receiving dividends above the dividend allowance, having income from savings or investments that has not been taxed at source, and making a capital gain above the annual exempt amount. Company directors also need to file if they have income outside PAYE, for example dividends from their own company, or rental income from a property they own personally.

There are also thresholds that catch people who think of themselves as employees. If your total income from employment is over £100,000, you must file a return, even if all of it was taxed through PAYE. The same applies if you or your partner claimed Child Benefit and either of you earned over £60,000, because the High Income Child Benefit Charge has to be reported through Self Assessment.

What Are the Self Assessment Deadlines?

The deadlines for Self Assessment are fixed, and they do not move. For the 2025/26 tax year, which ran from 6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026, the online return must reach HMRC by 11:59pm on 31 January 2027, and any tax you owe must be paid by the same time. The paper return deadline for the same year was 31 October 2026, three months earlier, so almost everyone files online.

If you have never filed before, you should have registered for Self Assessment by 5 October 2026, so that HMRC could issue your UTR in time. If you want HMRC to collect any tax you owe through your tax code, by adjusting your PAYE, you need to file by 30 December, but that only works if the tax owed is under £3,000 and you are employed or receive a pension.

The 31 January deadline covers three things at once: the return itself, the balancing payment for the year just ended, and the first payment on account towards the following year, where one is due.

How Do You File Your Self Assessment Tax Return Online Step by Step?

First, register if you have not already, and make sure you have your UTR and your Government Gateway or GOV.UK One Login details. Your UTR is the 10-digit Unique Taxpayer Reference HMRC issues when you register for Self Assessment. If this is your first return, do this weeks before the deadline, because the UTR arrives by post and cannot be hurried.

Next, gather your records: your income from self-employment, employment, rent, dividends, interest and any capital gains, and your allowable expenses, pension contributions and Gift Aid donations. If your records are kept in accounting software or a spreadsheet, export the totals for the tax year and check them against your bank statements.

Then sign in to HMRC online services and start the return for the right tax year. Work through each section in turn, entering the figures HMRC asks for, and do not skip a section because you think it does not apply.

Check the calculation before you submit it. HMRC shows you the tax it has worked out, including any payments on account you have already made and the balancing payment still due. Once you submit, keep the confirmation and a copy of the return, with the date, alongside your records. You can amend an online return within 12 months of the deadline if you find a mistake.

What Are Payments on Account?

Payments on account are advance payments towards next year's tax bill, and they are the part of Self Assessment that surprises people most. If your last tax bill was more than £1,000, and less than 80% of your tax was collected at source through PAYE, HMRC will ask you to pay next year's tax in two instalments, each normally 50% of the previous year's bill, due on 31 January and 31 July.

That means the bill on 31 January can be 150% of what you expected: the balancing payment for the year just ended, plus the first payment on account for the year you are now in. You can ask HMRC to reduce your payments on account if you know your income will be lower this year than last year. But be careful: if you reduce them by more than the tax actually due, HMRC charges interest on the shortfall, backdated to the date the payment was due.

If your bill was under £1,000, or more than 80% of your tax was collected through PAYE, you do not make payments on account at all. You simply pay the balancing payment by 31 January.

What Expenses Can a Small Business Claim?

You can claim the costs of running your business, so long as they were incurred wholly and exclusively for the business. The commonest allowable expenses for a small business are the costs of goods you sell, office costs such as stationery and phone bills, travel costs for business journeys, the cost of a business vehicle, staff costs, professional fees for accountants and solicitors, and the cost of advertising and marketing.

If you work from home, you can claim a proportion of your household costs, heating, electricity, internet and rent or mortgage interest, based on how much of your home you use for work and how often.

What you cannot claim is just as important. You cannot claim for your own salary as a sole trader. Your drawings are not a business expense. You cannot claim for personal spending, for fines and penalties, for entertaining clients beyond the narrow rules that apply, or for the cost of buying a property.

Keep receipts and records for every expense you claim, for at least five years after the 31 January filing deadline. HMRC can ask to see them, and an expense without a receipt is an expense you may have to repay, with interest.

What Happens If You File Your Self Assessment Late?

If you file late, the penalty is automatic, and it applies even if you owe no tax or are due a refund. One day late, the penalty is £100. That is the penalty for the paperwork being late, not for the tax being late.

Three months late, daily penalties of £10 a day begin, running for up to 90 days, so they add up to £900 on top of the £100. Six months late, there is a further penalty of 5% of the tax due or £300, whichever is greater. Twelve months late, another 5% or £300 applies, and in serious cases HMRC can charge up to 100% of the tax due.

Late payment is penalised separately. If the tax is still unpaid 30 days after the deadline, HMRC charges 5% of the unpaid tax, then another 5% at six months and another 5% at twelve months, plus interest from 1 February until the tax is paid.

If you have already missed the deadline, file as soon as you can. The penalty grows with how late the filing is, so filing a week late costs far less than filing three months late. If you cannot pay the tax you owe, contact HMRC about a Time to Pay arrangement, spreading the tax over monthly instalments, rather than leaving it unpaid while penalties and interest build up.

How Does Self Assessment Work for a Company Director?

A company director who takes a salary through PAYE and dividends from the company still needs to file a Self Assessment return if they have income that has not been taxed at source. Dividends are the commonest reason: dividends are not taxed through PAYE, so they have to be reported on the return, and the tax on them is worked out there.

The director's salary is reported on the return as employment income, with the tax already paid through PAYE shown alongside it. Dividends are reported separately, with the dividend allowance applied first.

The company's own tax is separate. The company pays Corporation Tax on its profits, and files a Company Tax Return with HMRC and accounts with Companies House. The director's Self Assessment return is personal: it covers the director's own income, not the company's. The two must agree: the dividends on the director's return must match the dividends the company's accounts show as paid.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Unique Taxpayer Reference (UTR)?

Your UTR is the 10-digit number HMRC issues when you register for Self Assessment. You cannot file without it. If you have never filed before, register first. It can take around 15 days for the UTR to arrive by post, so do not leave it until January.

Can I amend my Self Assessment return after I file it?

Yes. You can amend an online return within 12 months of the deadline. For 2025/26, that means by 31 January 2028. It is better to get it right the first time, but an amendment is possible if you find a mistake.

Do I need to file Self Assessment if I am a company director?

You need to file if you have income outside PAYE, such as dividends from your own company or rental income. The company's Corporation Tax return is separate and does not cover your personal income.

What records should I keep, and for how long?

Keep all records of your income and expenses for at least five years after the 31 January filing deadline for the tax year they relate to. From 6 April 2026, Making Tax Digital for Income Tax applies to sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000: they must keep digital records and send quarterly updates through compatible software.
