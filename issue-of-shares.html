Corporate Services > Issue of Shares

An issue of shares service helps a small limited company create new shares and give them to a new or existing shareholder: properly authorised, properly recorded, and filed with Companies House on time. Issuing shares is how a company brings in a new investor, rewards a co-founder or employee, or turns a loan into ownership, but it is not a step to take casually. New shares change who owns the company, how much control each shareholder has, and what the public register shows.

What Is an Issue of Shares Service?

An issue of shares service is the professional help a company uses when it allots new shares, that is, when it creates shares that did not exist before and gives them to someone. The service covers the whole process: checking that the directors have the authority to allot, preparing the board resolution, dealing with pre-emption rights, updating the statutory registers, issuing the share certificate, and filing the return of allotment, form SH01, with Companies House.

Issuing shares is different from the shares a company had when it was formed. At incorporation, the company issues its first shares to its founders, usually one or a few shares each, at a nominal value of £1. After that, every new share must be allotted through a proper process. The company cannot simply agree in a conversation that someone now owns 20% of the business; the allotment must be authorised, recorded in the company's registers, and reported to Companies House.

Why Would a Small Business Issue New Shares?

The most common reason is investment. A new investor puts money into the company in return for shares, so the company gets the cash it needs to grow and the investor gets a stake in the business. The shares are allotted at an agreed price, which is usually far above the £1 nominal value. The difference is the share premium, and it goes into the company's share premium account rather than being treated as profit.

Another common reason is bringing in a co-founder or a key employee. A company that wants a new director or a senior employee to have a real stake in the business will often allot them shares, sometimes at a low price or for no cash at all, as part of their package.

Shares are also issued to convert a loan into ownership. If a director or an investor has lent the company money, the company may agree to allot shares in place of repaying the loan. A company may also issue bonus shares, new shares given to existing shareholders for free, in proportion to the shares they already hold. Bonus shares do not bring in new money; they are paid for out of the company's reserves.

What Is the Difference Between Issuing Shares and Transferring Shares?

Issuing shares, also called allotting shares, creates new shares. The company's total number of shares goes up, and its share capital increases. A new shareholder joins, or an existing shareholder's holding grows, and every other shareholder's percentage of the company falls unless they also take new shares.

Transferring shares does not create anything new. An existing shareholder sells or gives their shares to someone else, and the total number of shares stays exactly the same. The buyer steps into the seller's shoes, and the company's share capital does not change. A transfer is recorded on a stock transfer form and in the register of members, and it does not need an SH01, because no new shares have been allotted.

For a small business, the practical point is simple: if the shares already exist and are changing hands, it is a transfer. If the company is creating shares that did not exist before, it is an issue, and the SH01 clock starts running.

Who Can Authorise an Issue of Shares?

Under the Companies Act 2006, the directors of a company may allot shares only if they are authorised to do so by the company's articles of association or by an ordinary resolution of the shareholders.

For many small private companies, the authority is already there. A private company with only one class of shares, formed under the Companies Act 2006 with the standard model articles, gives its directors a general authority to allot shares, unless the articles say otherwise. That is why the first step in any share issue is to read the articles: they may grant the authority, limit it, or take it away altogether.

Where the articles do not grant the authority, the shareholders must grant it by ordinary resolution, a resolution passed by more than half of the votes cast. That authorisation must state the maximum number of shares the directors may allot under it, and it may last for no more than five years. It can be renewed or revoked by a further ordinary resolution, and a copy of the resolution must be sent to Companies House within 15 days.

If the directors allot shares without the authority they need, the allotment may be invalid, and the directors may be personally liable.

What Are Pre-emption Rights, and Do They Apply?

Pre-emption rights are the existing shareholders' right of first refusal when new ordinary shares are issued. Under the Companies Act 2006, a company must not allot equity securities, broadly ordinary shares, to a person unless it has first offered them to each existing holder of ordinary shares, on the same or more favourable terms, in proportion to the shares they already hold.

The idea is straightforward: if you own 25% of a company, and the company issues new shares, you should have the chance to buy 25% of the new shares, so your percentage does not fall without your agreement. The company must give each shareholder a period to accept or refuse the offer, and it cannot allot the shares to anyone else until that period has expired or every shareholder has replied.

There are exceptions. Pre-emption rights do not apply to bonus shares, and they do not apply where the shares are paid for otherwise than in cash, for example where shares are issued in exchange for property or for the cancellation of a loan. A private company can also exclude pre-emption rights in its articles, or disapply them for a particular allotment by special resolution, which needs at least 75% of the votes cast.

What Are the 6 Things to Check Before You Issue Shares?

  1. Do the directors have authority to allot? Check the articles of association and any existing ordinary resolution. If the authority is missing or has expired, get the shareholders to grant it first, and remember the five-year maximum.
  2. Do pre-emption rights apply? Check the articles and any shareholders' agreement. If the existing shareholders have pre-emption rights, offer them the new shares first, or pass a special resolution to disapply the rights for this allotment.
  3. Is the price and the consideration agreed? Agree how much each new share will cost, whether it is paid in cash or otherwise, and how much of the price is nominal value and how much is share premium.
  4. Will the allotment change who controls the company? Work out the shareholdings after the issue. A new shareholder may become a person with significant control, which must be reported to Companies House.
  5. Are the registers and the statement of capital ready to be updated? The register of members and the statement of capital must show the position after the allotment, not before it.
  6. Is the one-month SH01 deadline in the diary? The SH01 must be filed within one month of the allotment, and the share certificates must be issued within two months.

How Do You Issue Shares Step by Step?

First, the directors check the authority and the pre-emption position, and agree the terms: who will receive the shares, how many, of what class, and at what price. If shareholder approval is needed, for the authority to allot or to disapply pre-emption rights, the shareholders pass the necessary resolution, and a copy is filed with Companies House within 15 days.

Next, the board passes a resolution to allot the shares. The minute records the number and class of shares, the allottee, the price, and the date of allotment. That date is important: it is the date from which the one-month SH01 deadline runs.

Then the allotment is recorded. The register of members is updated to show the new shareholder and their holding. The share certificate is prepared and must be issued within two months of the allotment.

Finally, the SH01 is filed with Companies House within one month of the allotment, with the updated statement of capital. The filing is usually made online, and it is the step that puts the new share capital on the public register. Filing the SH01 does not create the shares. The shares are created when the board resolves to allot them, but filing it is a legal duty, and the company and its officers can be fined if it is late.

What Is Form SH01 and the Statement of Capital?

Form SH01 is the return of allotment of shares. It is the form a company files with Companies House to report that it has allotted new shares after incorporation, and it must be filed within one month of the allotment under section 555 of the Companies Act 2006.

The form records the date of the allotment, the number and class of shares allotted, the nominal value of each share, the amount paid and unpaid on the shares, and the consideration received. It also records the rights attached to the shares, the prescribed particulars, where a new class of shares has been created.

The SH01 must be accompanied by a statement of capital. That statement is a snapshot of the company's entire issued share capital immediately after the allotment, not just the new shares. For each class of shares, it shows the total number of shares in issue, the aggregate nominal value, and the aggregate amount unpaid, plus the overall totals across all classes. It must reconcile with the register of members.

Getting the statement of capital wrong is the most common error on an SH01. Companies that show only the new shares, or that forget to update the totals, file a return that does not agree with their own registers. The statement of capital also appears on the company's annual confirmation statement, so an error in one filing tends to be carried into the next unless it is spotted and fixed.

How Much Does It Cost to Issue Shares?

There is no separate Companies House fee for filing an SH01 online, so the statutory cost of the filing is not the barrier. The real costs are the adviser's fee for checking the authority and pre-emption position, preparing the resolutions, updating the registers and filing the SH01, and, where the allotment is part of an investment, the legal cost of the subscription agreement or shareholders' agreement that records the investor's rights.

The price of the shares themselves is a separate matter. New shares are usually issued at a price above their nominal value, and the amount above nominal value is the share premium. If a company with 100 shares of £1 each issues 25 new shares at £100 each, the new shareholder pays £2,500: £25 of nominal value and £2,475 of share premium. That money belongs to the company, and it is not a fee. It is the investment the company receives for the shares.

What Happens If You Issue Shares Incorrectly or File SH01 Late?

If shares are issued incorrectly, the problem is not only the filing, it is the shares themselves. An allotment made without the authority the directors needed, or in breach of pre-emption rights, can be challenged, and the new shareholder may not have a secure title to the shares.

A late SH01 is a separate offence. The return must be filed within one month of the allotment, and failing to file on time is an offence by the company and every officer in default, punishable by a fine and a daily default fine while the failure continues. The allotment itself is still valid, but the late filing is visible on the public register.

A wrong SH01 causes a different problem: the public register shows the wrong share capital. The company's own register of members will not agree with Companies House, and the confirmation statement, which repeats the statement of capital, will carry the error forward. Correcting it needs a second filing, and the original, incorrect filing stays on the register alongside the correction.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between issuing shares and transferring shares?

Issuing shares creates new shares, so the company's total number of shares goes up. Transferring shares moves existing shares from one person to another, and the total stays exactly the same. An issue needs an SH01. A transfer does not.

How long do directors have to file Form SH01?

Form SH01 must be filed within one month of the allotment. Share certificates must be issued within two months of the allotment.

Is there a Companies House fee for filing SH01?

No. There is no separate Companies House fee for filing an SH01 online. The cost is the professional cost of doing it properly, plus the money the new shareholder pays for the shares.

What are pre-emption rights?

Pre-emption rights give existing shareholders the first chance to buy new shares, in proportion to their existing holdings, so their percentage of the company does not fall without their agreement. A private company can exclude them in its articles, or disapply them for a particular allotment by special resolution.
