How to File Dormant Accounts (AA02) at Companies House

What dormant means, who can use Form AA02, and what happens if the filing is late.

Corporate Services > Dormant Accounts

Filing dormant accounts on Form AA02 is the short, free filing a company that has never traded uses to tell Companies House it had no significant accounting transactions in the year. A dormant company may have done nothing at all, no sales, no expenses, no bank activity, but doing nothing does not mean filing nothing. The accounts are still due every year, on the same timetable as a trading company, and missing them brings the same automatic penalties.

What Does It Mean for a Company to Be Dormant?

A company is dormant, for Companies House purposes, when it has had no significant accounting transactions during its financial year. That definition comes from section 1169 of the Companies Act 2006, and it is narrower than most people expect.

Most dormant companies fall into a few groups. A company may have been formed for an idea that has not started yet. A company may have been set up to hold an asset, such as a property, without trading. Or a company may have been kept on the register after a project ended, because the owners want to keep the name or restart the business later.

A dormant company is still a live company. It still has directors, and those directors still have legal duties. It still has a registered office, a set of statutory registers, and a filing calendar. The only thing that has paused is the trading.

It is also worth keeping the two definitions of dormant apart, because Companies House and HMRC do not use the same one. For Companies House, the test is whether there were significant accounting transactions. For HMRC, a company is dormant for Corporation Tax purposes when it is not trading and has no income, and HMRC counts bank interest as income.

What Counts as a Significant Accounting Transaction?

A significant accounting transaction is any transaction that has to be entered in the company's accounting records, other than a small list of exceptions. The exceptions are the ones that exist only to set the company up: the issue of subscriber shares when the company is formed, the payment of the fee to incorporate the company, and the payment of a fee to Companies House for filing a document, such as the confirmation statement fee.

Almost everything else counts. Paying rent counts. Buying stock or equipment counts. Paying a salary, a director's fee or an accountant counts. Receiving money from a customer counts, and so does receiving bank interest. Even a small payment out of the company bank account, if it is a business payment, is a significant accounting transaction. The size of the transaction does not matter: a £5 expense is still a transaction that must be recorded, and it still breaks dormancy for that period.

This is where small companies most often go wrong. A founder pays for a domain name from the company account, or a bank charge is taken, or a few pounds of interest arrive, and the company is no longer dormant for that year, even though nobody thinks of it as trading.

Who Can File Dormant Accounts on Form AA02?

Form AA02 is the simplified dormant company accounts form, and it is only available to a narrow group of companies. To use it, the company must be limited by shares, it must never have traded, and the only transaction in its accounting records must be the issue of subscriber shares. It must also not be a subsidiary of another company.

A company limited by guarantee cannot use AA02 at all, even if it has never traded. A company that traded at any point, even briefly in an earlier year, cannot use AA02 once it goes dormant. It has to continue filing the accounts that fit its size, usually micro-entity or small company accounts, for as long as it stays on the register.

For a company that does qualify, AA02 asks for very little. It is a balance-sheet-only filing: the balance sheet date, the total value of called-up share capital, and the net assets figure. There is no profit and loss account, no directors' report, and no auditor is needed.

How Do You File Dormant Accounts Step by Step?

First, confirm that the company qualifies for AA02, using the checks below. If it has never traded, is limited by shares, is not a subsidiary, and has had no significant accounting transactions, it qualifies.

Next, gather the three things the filing needs: the balance sheet date, the called-up share capital figure, and the Companies House authentication code. The balance sheet date is the company's accounting reference date, the last day of its financial year. The share capital figure is the total value of the shares issued to the subscribers when the company was formed. The authentication code is the code Companies House issued for the company.

Then sign in to the Companies House WebFiling service and choose the dormant accounts filing. Enter the balance sheet date, the share capital and the net assets, and confirm the statement that the company was dormant for the period. Check every figure before submitting, because the filing goes onto the public record.

There is no Companies House fee for filing dormant accounts online. The AA02 is free.

What Are the 6 Checks to Make Before You File Dormant Accounts?

  1. Has the company really had no significant accounting transactions? Check the bank statements and the accounting records for the whole period. A single payment, a bank charge or a receipt of interest breaks dormancy for that year.
  2. Has the company ever traded? AA02 is only for a company that has never traded. If it traded in an earlier year and then stopped, it must file micro-entity or small company accounts instead.
  3. Is the company limited by shares, and not a subsidiary? A company limited by guarantee cannot use AA02, and neither can a subsidiary.
  4. Do you have the Companies House authentication code? If the code has been lost, request a new one. It takes about five days to arrive by post, so do not leave it until the deadline week.
  5. Is the balance sheet date right? The date on the AA02 must match the end of the company's financial year. Filing with the wrong date is one of the commonest reasons a filing is rejected.
  6. Is the share capital figure right? The called-up share capital on the form must match the shares issued to the subscribers at incorporation, and the net assets figure must agree with it.

When Are Dormant Accounts Due?

Dormant accounts follow the same filing deadlines as ordinary accounts. For a private limited company, accounts must reach Companies House within nine months of the accounting reference date, the last day of the company's financial year. Being dormant does not extend this.

For a new company, the first set of accounts has a little more time. The first accounts are due 21 months after incorporation, or three months after the accounting reference date, whichever gives the longer period.

The deadline is the date the accounts must reach Companies House, not the date they are started, posted or approved. An online filing made on the deadline day counts. A paper filing posted on the deadline day does not, if it arrives late.

What Must You Still File While Your Company Is Dormant?

Dormancy does not pause the confirmation statement. Every company, trading or not, has to file one at least once every 12 months, confirming that the company's registered details are current. The online filing fee is £50, and the paper fee is £110. A dormant company pays the same fee as a trading one.

The confirmation statement and the dormant accounts are separate filings, with separate deadlines, and one does not cover the other. The company's statutory registers must also be kept up to date, even while it is dormant. If a director changes, if the registered office moves, or if the shareholding changes, Companies House must be told on the relevant form, within the usual time limits.

What Do You Need to Tell HMRC About a Dormant Company?

Companies House does not tell HMRC anything on your behalf. If you want HMRC to stop expecting a Corporation Tax return, you need to tell them directly that the company is not trading and has no income. Until you do, HMRC will keep issuing notices to file, and a notice that is ignored can lead to a determination and a penalty, even for a company that owes no tax.

Tell HMRC the company is dormant for Corporation Tax as soon as it stops trading, or as soon as it is formed if it has not started trading. The moment the company starts trading again, or starts earning taxable income such as bank interest, you need to notify HMRC within three months of that happening, and then resume filing Corporation Tax returns as normal.

The penalty is automatic: Companies House does not send a warning first, and it does not waive the penalty because the company was dormant or because nothing was owed.

A late filing also has a second consequence. Persistent non-filing can lead to the company being struck off the register. If a company is no longer needed, the right step is to close it properly, not to stop filing. Bring the accounts and the confirmation statement up to date, then apply to strike the company off with form DS01, which costs £13 online or £18 by paper.

If accounts are already late, file them as soon as possible. The penalty is charged by how late the filing is, so filing a week late costs less than filing three months late.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is filing dormant accounts free?

Yes. There is no Companies House fee for filing dormant accounts online on Form AA02. The cost is the adviser's fee, where one is used, for checking the position and making the filing.

Does a dormant company still need to file a confirmation statement?

Yes. Dormancy does not pause the confirmation statement. Every company must file one at least once every 12 months, and a dormant company pays the same £50 online fee as a trading one.

Can a company that once traded file dormant accounts?

No. Form AA02 is only for a company that has never traded. A company that traded in an earlier year and then stopped must file micro-entity or small company accounts instead, even if the current year was completely quiet.

How do you wake a dormant company up again?

Tell HMRC within three months of the company starting to trade or earning taxable income. Then file full statutory accounts at the next Companies House deadline, rather than the simplified dormant accounts, and check VAT, payroll and the company's registers.
